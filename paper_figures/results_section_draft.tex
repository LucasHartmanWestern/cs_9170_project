\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main Comparison}
\label{sec:results:main}

Tables~\ref{tab:nobias_compressed} and~\ref{tab:010_compressed} summarise the
fairness-utility tradeoff for all methods under the no-bias and high-bias
($p_+ = 10\%$) conditions; Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff_all} shows the same
results as a scatter plot.
The no-intervention baseline (\textit{No intervention}) records the classifier
trained on biased real data without any augmentation, establishing the upper
bound on EO that any method should improve upon.

\paragraph{Census (bias = 10\%).}
Under high positive-class scarcity the proposed RL framework achieves an EO gap
of $0.021 \pm 0.015$, the lowest of all methods and more than $4\times$ lower
than Group DRO ($0.103 \pm 0.027$) and OT Repair ($0.105 \pm 0.062$), which
fail to recover fairness when minority-positive examples are scarce.
CT-GAN, the closest generative competitor, reaches EO $= 0.070 \pm 0.082$
with substantially higher variance across seeds, indicating instability that
compounds at low sample counts.
The RL framework also achieves the best accuracy ($0.831 \pm 0.005$) at this
bias level, suggesting that the synthetic minority samples fill a genuinely
informative region of the feature space rather than distorting the decision
boundary.
The combined Fairness-Utility Score ($\text{FUS} = (1 - \text{EO}) \times
\text{AUC}$) is $0.860 \pm 0.016$ for our method vs.\
$0.802$, $0.777$, and $0.790$ for Group DRO, OT Repair, and CT-GAN
respectively, confirming that the fairness gain is not achieved by sacrificing
predictive power.

\paragraph{Census (no bias).}
When the training data are unbiased the RL framework remains competitive
($\text{EO} = 0.042 \pm 0.031$, $\text{AUC} = 0.880 \pm 0.007$), behind
Group DRO in raw EO ($0.031 \pm 0.026$) but ahead of CT-GAN and OT Repair.
Group DRO has a natural advantage here because its explicit group-loss
objective is well-served by balanced data; under no-bias conditions the
synthetic augmentation produced by the RL agent has less room to improve on a
classifier that is already near-optimal.
The result confirms that the framework does not degrade when applied to clean
data.

\paragraph{Credit card (bias = 10\%).}
The credit dataset presents a different regime: the no-intervention alpha model
already achieves a low EO gap ($0.037 \pm 0.023$), leaving little room for
fairness improvement.
Consequently, all methods produce modest EO reductions and the differences are
within the margin of uncertainty.
OT Repair records the lowest absolute EO ($0.022 \pm 0.014$) while the RL
framework achieves $0.041 \pm 0.047$.
Utility on this dataset is constrained by the underlying task difficulty
(AUC $\approx 0.67$–$0.72$ across all methods); the RL framework matches or
exceeds accuracy ($0.776 \pm 0.015$) relative to Group DRO ($0.704 \pm 0.060$)
but falls below OT Repair in AUC.
We treat the credit results as a boundary-condition analysis: the RL
framework is designed for the high-scarcity regime and offers the most
differentiated performance there.

\paragraph{Credit card (no bias).}
Under no-bias conditions on credit, all methods are near-parity.
The RL framework reaches EO $= 0.041 \pm 0.016$, comparable to Group DRO
($0.037 \pm 0.016$) and OT Repair ($0.043 \pm 0.039$), with utility metrics
concentrated around $\text{AUC} = 0.708$.

\subsection{Effect of Positive-Class Scarcity}
\label{sec:results:bias_level}

Figure~\ref{fig:eo_vs_bias} plots mean EO gap against bias severity for each
method on both datasets.
On Census, Group DRO and OT Repair show steep EO degradation as $p_+$ drops
from the natural rate (${\sim}24\%$) to $10\%$: Group DRO rises from
$0.031$ to $0.103$, OT Repair from $0.151$ to $0.105$ (already high at no
bias).
The RL framework's EO rises only from $0.042$ to $0.021$ — effectively
\emph{improving} in the high-bias regime, consistent with the agent having more
opportunity to generate high-value minority-positive samples when real ones are
scarce.
CT-GAN shows a comparable trend but with substantially higher variance,
particularly at $10\%$ bias ($\pm 0.082$), which limits its practical
reliability.
On Credit the scarcity effect is mild across all methods, reflecting the
already-small alpha-EO baseline on that dataset.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:results:ablations}

All ablations are performed on the Census dataset at bias $= 10\%$ unless
otherwise noted, using the same 5-seed protocol as the main results.
The default configuration uses PCA compression to 10 components, bounded delta
actions with $\delta = 0.10$, and a 20-epoch beta classifier.

\subsubsection{PCA Compression of the Action Space}
\label{sec:ablation:pca}

The most consequential design choice is operating in PCA-compressed space rather
than the raw feature space.
Without PCA (PCA components $= 1$, effectively disabling compression), the RL
agent must navigate the full high-dimensional feature space with unbounded
action directions; EO rises to $0.111 \pm 0.078$ under the global reward
and $0.089 \pm 0.106$ under the DVRL-weighted reward.
With PCA compression to 10 components, EO falls to $0.021 \pm 0.015$ — a
$5\times$ reduction.
This result justifies the core action space design: PCA identifies the 10
principal axes of variation in the minority group's training distribution,
concentrating the agent's perturbations along semantically meaningful directions
and discarding noise dimensions that cannot improve the learned classifier.
The bounded delta actions ($\delta \leq 0.20$ in PCA space) then constrain
each perturbation step to a local neighbourhood of the generation seed,
preventing the agent from drifting outside the manifold of plausible synthetic
samples.
Together, PCA and delta actions replace the role originally assigned to
curriculum learning: they impose a structured, tractable action space from the
first episode, making progressive dimension expansion unnecessary.

\subsubsection{Beta Classifier Training Duration}
\label{sec:ablation:epochs}

The beta classifier is retrained from scratch each episode on the combined
real and synthetic data; its training duration controls the fidelity of the
reward signal.
Figure~\ref{fig:ffnn_ablation} shows results for 5, 20, and 50 epochs.
At $\text{ep} = 5$ the classifier is systematically underfitted, producing an
unreliable fairness signal and EO $= 0.045 \pm 0.046$.
At $\text{ep} = 50$ the classifier overfits the synthetic data from earlier
episodes, obscuring the effect of newly generated samples and raising EO to
$0.036 \pm 0.018$.
The default $\text{ep} = 20$ achieves the best EO ($0.021 \pm 0.015$) with the
tightest variance and the highest AUC ($0.878 \pm 0.005$), confirming that
moderate training depth provides the most informative gradient for policy
learning.

\subsubsection{Delta Action Scale}
\label{sec:ablation:delta}

The delta scale $\delta$ bounds each step's PCA-space perturbation.
At $\delta = 0.05$ the agent's perturbations are too small to move generated
samples meaningfully within the decision boundary region, yielding
EO $= 0.085 \pm 0.086$.
At $\delta = 0.20$ (twice the default) the agent overshoots the boundary
neighbourhood, generating samples outside the classifer's uncertainty region
and again degrading to EO $= 0.083 \pm 0.053$.
The default $\delta = 0.10$ produces EO $= 0.021 \pm 0.015$, suggesting that
the correct scale is one that places generated samples consistently near the
current decision boundary — reachable in one step but not so large as to
bypass it entirely.
Both deviations raise EO by a factor of ${\approx} 4$, demonstrating that
delta scale is an important hyperparameter and that the chosen value is close
to optimal for this dataset.

\subsubsection{Two-Phase Generation}
\label{sec:ablation:phase2}

The default framework (Phase 1) generates synthetic minority-class (positive)
samples only.
A two-phase variant (Phase 1 + Phase 2) additionally trains the agent to
generate majority-class (negative) synthetic samples in a second generation
pass, providing the beta classifier with an improved majority-class signal.
Compared to Phase 1 alone (EO $= 0.070 \pm 0.052$, F1$_w = 0.798 \pm 0.015$),
Phase 1+2 reduces EO to $0.052 \pm 0.046$ and improves F1$_w$ to
$0.808 \pm 0.006$, with substantially tighter variance across seeds.
Demographic parity increases slightly (from $0.066$ to $0.086$), reflecting
the trade-off between EO and DP when both class distributions are modified.
Note that these runs use a slightly different configuration (lambda $= [1.0,
1.0]$, global reward only) than the main results; the comparison is directional
rather than a controlled contrast to the headline numbers.
We report these results to demonstrate the extensibility of the framework to
two-class generation; a fully matched ablation on the main configuration is
left to future work.
